### Case Study — Ivy Prep (FINAL, verbatim — do not edit)

At Ivy Prep, I worked with two very different kinds of student writing. One was UCAS and UK-bound applications, where the final edit and review mattered most, tightening what was already largely there. The other was US summer school applications, where students had to write about their academic and extracurricular achievements, and, more importantly, about the process behind them: what the experience actually taught them, and how it changed them. This mattered more for US applications specifically, where growth as a person carries as much weight as the achievement itself, versus the more quantitative expectations of UK applications.

Most students wrote a first draft after a session of brainstorming, and I'd come back with specific feedback on the parts that needed it, along with minor tweaks of my own. One recurring problem was AI. So many students had grown used to leaning on it that their stories started sounding alike, flattened into the same tone and structure regardless of who was actually writing. Catching that, and pushing students back toward their own voice, became a consistent part of the feedback I gave.

That problem is part of why the John Locke Institute essay stands out. I worked with three students on their submissions, one in economics, one in philosophy or political relations, and one in psychology. The question for psychology was: why do we care for our bodies after death? It was her first academic argumentative essay, let alone for a competition of that calibre, so before we even touched the topic, we spent a session or two just on academic writing: structure, argument, language.

We started circling ideas around body, mind, soul, religion, the obvious territory. But she wanted a different angle, so I took her through post-humanist theory, Foucault and Derrida, ideas of power, control and conditioning, and we built an argument around how institutions of power condition people to care for their bodies, and why. We backed it with quantitative evidence to give the argument weight, and stuck strictly to academic sources for citations, even where we'd skimmed other material along the way. After the first draft, we reviewed it together, tightened it for readability, and prepared a second. Then came the final citations, the reference list, and two full proofreads before submission. AI had almost no role in any of it. In June, she was shortlisted for the academic conference in London, one of a small number chosen per category to present before a winner is picked.

The Undergraduate Journal Program ran alongside this. It's a three-part series, each part built around a different aspect of a student's life, meant to document their stories and achievements in their own words, so that by the time they sit down to write their actual application essays (eight per college), they already have material to draw from instead of starting cold. Most students had simply never been asked the kind of questions the journal poses, so a lot of the work was helping them brainstorm, sometimes one-on-one, sometimes in joint sessions with parents and the head counsellor, to get real depth out of their answers. Each part ran on a strict timeline, which I tracked, following up with students who were falling behind to help them find the time and still give considered answers rather than rushed ones.

The blog-building work came in almost as an afterthought, but I enjoyed it more than expected. I set up a student's WordPress account and built her site from scratch, then worked with her to figure out what to actually write about: what she liked, what she was good at, what shaped her. We wrote her About Me page together, then moved into blog posts about her life as a teenager, the classes she'd taken, trips she'd been on. She'd barely written before this and was quiet, an introvert who found it genuinely difficult to open up on the page, but she was self-driven, and after a few sessions, she started producing good, honest writing on her own.

By May 2026, when I left Ivy Prep to begin my Master's, the work had taken a few different shapes, essay editing, academic coaching, a journal program, even a blog built from nothing, but the thread running through all of it was the same: helping a student find a version of their own voice that was actually theirs.

### Case Study — XAP (FINAL, verbatim — do not edit)

An interview is more than just getting someone to talk, it's also about maintaining their humanity. Especially if they are people like Rekha ben, Jyotshna ben, and Lakshmi ben, members of Xavier's housekeeping staff. We decided to interview them for the November 2025 issue, the first issue of the rebuild, and I knew that we had to ensure that they felt safe when speaking with us. I wrote the questions for the interview and mediated the conversation myself, not to lead it but to steer it. We asked them about their lives, letting them speak as working women with routines, dreams, and dignity, not as a human-interest footnote, or a sympathy section. This interview for me was more than extracting a story, and more about respecting the storyteller — the kind of judgement I care the most about bringing to any piece of editing.

In 2024, I joined XAP as a Junior Editor. I was mostly reading, shortlisting submissions and sending them to the Senior Editors for the final issue. But this changed fast, in July when Xavier's hosted a three-day inter-college Youth Festival and the paper turned into a live newsroom filling copy from more than eight venues, our team editing and laying it out same-day for morning prints. Our principal even called us a "mini-daily" of Xavier's, something I value immensely.

By the time I became a Senior Editor, then the only one left after my co-editors graduated, I inherited a team of ten and a paper that hadn't yet built the systems a growing publication needed. We were running thin on sponsorships, had tight timelines, and writing that didn't always land with the readers. Instead of recruiting more members to work, I rebuilt the structure of the team giving two Junior Editors the lead as Senior Editors and Marketing and Design leads respectively. I recruited new members into the newly structured team with three divisions over two days, and at the end we had a team of 25 students. I stayed closest to the editorial core, and deliberately handed real ownership to others where I could; the Social Media team, once they found their footing, pulled over 120,000 views off two reels entirely on their own instinct, which only happened because I stepped back rather than staying in the middle of it.

The editorial judgement mattered more than the org chart. For the June 2026 issue, a faculty head from ECC sent us an article that was entirely AI-generated. It was polished, but hollow, with none of her actual voice in it. I rewrote it from scratch using her real quotes and input instead, because a periodical built on student and staff voices can't run something that isn't, in any real sense, hers. It's a small decision, but it's the same principle I try to bring to my working style: the tool can assist, the judgement and the voice have to stay human.

The hardest editorial call I made was in an interview with Nandini Ranpara, marketing head (2023-2025) and ECC head (2025-2026). The tone in the first two drafts, written by our interviewer, didn't match the work she'd actually done, and read like it was undermining her rather than representing her fairly. I sent it back twice. When the tone still wasn't right on the third pass, I made the call to hand it to someone else entirely rather than publish something that painted Nandini in a bad light. It cost us time and a full rewrite, but the alternative was running a piece that misrepresented someone who'd trusted us with her story.

Some of my best editorial decisions were smaller and more structural. On the November 2025 issue's spread on media and misogyny, I curated the pull-quotes at the bottom of the page myself (lines from Napoleon, Plato), deliberately placing celebrated, "respected" men's words about women directly beneath the articles, so the contradiction did the arguing instead of the copy having to state it outright. On page six of the June 2026 issue, I balanced tone across the page letting a couple of pieces run conversational and Hinglish-inflected, with titles like "enemies to lovers," so the two more serious pieces beside them would land harder by contrast rather than getting lost in a wall of heavy reading.

I wrote, too, not just edited. The November issue's opening piece, on womanhood at Xavier's and in general, was mine — continued a page later under "Oh Woman, Where Do You Come From?" I interviewed our then-principal, Fr. Dr. David Roy, myself, edited the interview with our Vice Principal, Dr. Mallika Sanyal, choosing the callout quote that anchored the page. Earlier, as Junior Editor, I wrote a full piece on India's diversity for the August–September issue, captioned a spread on bizarre festivals from around the world for October, and reviewed a politically charged film for November 2024 without letting the review become an argument about politics it didn't need to have.

Both the November 2025 and June 2026 issues carry my layout work too. I sketched the page structure for each, planned space for late-breaking articles and ads, and made the calls on what ran where. When I graduated, I left a team that could run without me. But if I had to point to the actual work, it's not the structure. It's a housekeeping interview handled with care, an AI-written article that got a human voice back, and an interview I refused to publish until it did right by the person in it.
